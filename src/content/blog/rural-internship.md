## Why I Joined

For most rural internships in college, it is simply a checkbox to tick off to meet degree requirements. There is nothing wrong with that,they do they. But to me, this was an opportunity. An opportunity to explore parts of me that I may have suppressed because of the programming this world had done to my mind.

This was my chance to view a world I had minimal exposure to because I spent my developing years outside India. I never really got to see the kind of life that exists on the other side, nor did I think much about ways I could contribute to this world or express care and empathy in a meaningful way.

This was my chance to join the **Unnat Bharat Abhiyan** by the Government of India, meant to bridge the gap between urban and rural communities. So I joined forces with four of my friends (Smit, Sanvi, Mishul, Laher) to spend twenty-eight days teaching English at a government school in Prantiya village.

## One Classroom Contained Ten Classrooms

Right after conducting my first lecture, I knew this classroom had a lot of diversity. And when I say a lot, I mean it.There were students failing to recognise the difference between **A** and **B**, while sitting a few benches away was another student who could spell almost any word if you simply pronounced it aloud.

That made me realise something interesting. "Grade 6" or "Grade 7" didn't really tell me much about where that child actually was academically. Those labels were almost meaningless. Inside one classroom there were kids who needed completely different starting points. Some could jump straight into forming sentences, while a few still needed someone to sit beside them and convince them that learning the alphabet was worth the effort because without it English would always feel impossible.

Fortunately, because there were five of us, we had the freedom to try something that a normal classroom usually cannot. Instead of one teacher trying to manage thirty students at once, each of us could take around six children. Sometimes one of us would teach a common concept to everyone, and a few minutes later we would split up again into smaller groups.

The more I thought about it, the more I realised this wasn't really an English problem. It was a teaching problem. Some kids needed phonics, some needed handwriting, some needed confidence, and some simply needed someone to slow down enough to meet them where they were. That was probably the first time I appreciated why personalised learning isn't just another educational buzzword. Once you've sat in front of a classroom like this, it almost becomes unavoidable.

## The Surprise

Before I stepped into this school, I had a lot of assumptions about how this was going to go, as with anything new you do for the first time. I expected these kids to be rude, mischievous, and play-oriented. Mostly negative words, if I'm being honest. But Amishal Modi, our coordinator for the program who had started teaching a couple of days earlier, had to say otherwise. She told us these kids were incredibly curious and eager to learn. It sounded counterintuitive to me. Still, after just one hour inside that school, I had to agree with her. There surely wasn't any lack of curiosity. If anything, it was the opposite. They were eager enough to jump out of their seats to answer questions, and many of them simply told me they loved studying.

Another thing that caught me off guard was how quickly these kids got attached to us. Within just a couple of days, one group of little girls had somehow adopted Sanvi and Laher. They kept asking us if they could "keep them" in the village and whether we would come visit their homes on Sunday. Another group kept insisting we give them Amishal ma'am's phone number. At first I laughed because I thought they were joking. After a while, I realised they weren't.

## Choosing the Bottom 20%

After discussing skill-based grouping, I decided to work with the bottom 20% of the class. I had a reason for that choice. I realised I enjoyed teaching one child at a time much more than teaching an entire classroom. It felt almost like solving a puzzle. I would observe where a child got stuck, identify the prerequisite they were missing, and then break that problem down into the smallest exercise they could successfully practice. Not too easy that they became bored. Not too difficult that they gave up. Just somewhere in that sweet spot.

I genuinely believe we can learn almost anything, provided we are taught the right way. I also had a personal belief that the brighter students would probably continue learning regardless of who stood in front of the classroom. But the students sitting at the bottom, the ones who felt left behind, those were the ones whose trajectory could genuinely change if someone invested enough time in them. If, after these twenty-eight days, I could simply help them catch up enough to feel confident again, I felt that impact would stay with them much longer.

Over the next four weeks, our mornings slowly settled into a rhythm.

For 45 minutes we would stop being "teachers" and simply become people they wanted to talk to. They would show us random things, tell us stories that made absolutely no sense, share the _chikki_ they got from "sarkar", ask us where DAU was, or simply stand around watching us.
Looking back, I think a lot of the trust we built happened then.

## How My Idea of Teaching Changed

Because of this Internship, I had started looking at teaching very differently. Initially, I thought teaching was mostly about explaining concepts clearly. But somewhere along those twenty-eight days, I started thinking of it more like an art form, one that can be mastered through practice, and at the same time something that can be studied using the scientific method.

This shouldn't really come as a surprise if you know me. Whenever I become interested in something, I usually end up reading research papers about it. So just on the third night after the internship had started, I found myself reading papers like [_Teaching the Science of Learning_](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780548/). Around the same time, I also came across [Spencer Russell](https://www.instagram.com/toddlerscanread/reels/), a teacher online who focuses on teaching very young kids how to read English using phonics and strong foundations.

Naturally, I began to wonder. Could I try out some of these ideas the very next day? Every day slowly became a small experiment. I found myself borrowing concepts like [Deliberate Practice](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practice_(learning_method)#Deliberate_practice>), the [Socratic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_method), and [Flow](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology)>), often without even realising it at the time. Sometimes ma'am would prepare what looked like a brilliant worksheet, only for the classroom to become noisy within five minutes. Other times, the exact same students would quietly spend half an hour solving sheet after sheet without us needing to tell them anything.

That made me wonder: what was the difference? Eventually I realised it usually came down to one thing, difficulty. When the work was too easy, they got bored. When it was too difficult, they gave up. But somewhere in between there was this sweet spot where they became completely absorbed in the activity. Looking back, this is probably where I unknowingly stumbled upon the idea of the Flow Channel.

![Flow channel illustration](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Florian-Bruehlmann/publication/279749323/figure/fig2/AS:391487863967751@1470349388913/The-flow-channel-is-a-balance-between-challenge-and-skill-Anxiety-arises-when-challenges.png)

Of course, not every day went according to plan. Some days it was Saturday. Some days it was Free Dress Day. Some days the kids were simply too excited about sports or something happening later that morning. On those days we had to stop thinking like people trying to finish a lesson plan and start thinking like teachers. Maybe today's lesson becomes a game. Maybe we spend more time revising instead of introducing something new. Maybe today's goal is simply keeping everyone engaged. Those failures were learnable too.

After a few weeks of trial and error, I realised my own teaching process had become surprisingly simple.

> Give the child an instruction.
>
> Watch carefully how they attempt it.
>
> Figure out exactly where they get stuck.
>
> Then design the smallest possible exercise that removes that obstacle on the spot.
>
> Repeat.

What fascinated me most was that the obstacle was rarely what I initially thought it was. Sometimes it wasn't spelling, it was letter formation. Sometimes it wasn't vocabulary, it was phonics. Sometimes they knew the answer perfectly well but simply lacked the confidence to say it aloud. Once I started thinking this way, teaching stopped feeling like standing in front of a classroom and started feeling like solving tiny little puzzles.

Another thing I kept noticing was that every time I taught the exact same lesson to another section later in the day, I became a noticeably better teacher than I had been two hours earlier. I already knew where students would get confused because I had just watched another class struggle with the same thing. That made me wonder how much refinement happens over an entire teaching career. If I could noticeably improve one lesson after teaching it twice, imagine someone who has taught that same lesson hundreds of times. Experienced teachers probably aren't just better at explaining. They've simply seen almost every mistake before. They quietly remove those roadblocks before students even reach them.

Somewhere around the second week I also started wondering whether software could replicate some part of this process. Not replace teachers, just help children through these tiny personalised progressions. Every child seemed to be stuck at a different point, and every child needed a slightly different next exercise. That felt like a problem computers are surprisingly good at solving.

I later came across [Duolingo ABC](https://abc.duolingo.com/) and realised people were already thinking along similar lines. Maybe one day I'll build something in this space myself, Maybe fine-tune an LLM for this task. 🤔

## Some Wholesome / Funny Moments

Some days everything went exactly as planned. Other days we walked into class and immediately realised that forcing today's worksheet wasn't going to work. Maybe it was Saturday. Maybe it was Free Dress Day. Maybe they were simply too excited about sports later that morning. We would improvise, turn the lesson into a game, host a long jump competition, or simply spend more time playing educational games than originally planned. Surprisingly, those were often the days the kids remembered the most.

There were also so many tiny moments that still randomly pop into my head.

One day a girl kept asking me to say "Banko." The classroom was so noisy that I misheard it as "Pankho" and confidently repeated it. The whole class burst into laughter. They made me say it again... and again... probably ten times before finally explaining what the word actually meant. I joined them because honestly, watching them laugh that hard was worth embarrassing myself a little.

Another day, one of the classroom dares was to do a mimicry of someone. The kids chose me. The boy stood up, pointed his finger exactly the way I usually do, and started asking everyone for their ABCs in that slightly commanding tone I apparently have. I didn't even realise I had developed those little teaching habits until I saw someone imitate them.

Then there was probably my favourite worksheet answer of the entire internship. The exercise had a picture of a cat beside it, and one kid proudly wrote, **"I touch with my cat."** Grammatically it made absolutely no sense, but somehow the confidence with which it was written made it even funnier. We kept laughing about that sentence long after the class had ended.

Towards the end of the internship, we were teaching opposite words. I asked the class what the _virodharthi_ (opposite) of **small** was. Without even thinking, the whole class shouted **"Capital!"** It took me a second before I realised why. For weeks we had been teaching them "small letters" and "capital letters." It was technically the wrong answer, but from their perspective it was probably the most logical answer they could have given.

Looking back, these are the moments I remember the most. Not because they were part of the lesson plan, but because they happened naturally. Somewhere along the way, the classroom stopped feeling like a place where we simply came to teach English. It became a place where all of us were learning, laughing, and making memories together.

## The Farewell

The last week of the internship felt different.

Up until then, our goal had always been to teach as much as we could during the limited time we had with these kids. But now we started thinking about what we could leave behind after we were gone. We decided to bring along some small gifts, animal stickers, board games, colouring books, juice, and cake. Nothing extravagant, just something to make the last few days memorable. Amishal ma'am was kind enough to sponsor the meal, and together we were able to celebrate with around 140 students.

![alt text](/assets/images/IMG-20260713-WA0035.jpg)![alt text](/assets/images/IMG-20260713-WA0032.jpg)

What surprised me most wasn't that the kids enjoyed the gifts, it was how grateful they were for them. Some of the children who picked up the colouring books kept asking us over and over,

> "Aa ghare lai jai hakya?"
>
> "Pachi aa pachhu nai aapvanu?"

They couldn't believe the books actually belonged to them. Watching them carefully hold onto those books as if they were something precious made me realise how differently value can be perceived depending on circumstance.

The farewell itself was much more emotional than I expected. A few students voluntarily came to the front of the classroom and thanked us, Amishal ma'am, and the rest of the team. As teachers, you rarely get immediate feedback on whether you've actually made any difference. Hearing those few words from children we had only known for four weeks somehow made all the early mornings, skipped breakfasts, difficult classes, and failed lesson plans feel completely worth it.

One funny thing about the last week was that we had accidentally said goodbye multiple times already. We taught different sections on different days, so every day we'd tell one group, _"This is our last class,"_ only to show up again the next morning for another section. By the actual final day, saying goodbye almost felt awkward because we'd already done it so many times.

Before leaving, we asked every student to write down everything they remembered from the past twenty-eight days. Some drew the landscapes we had once sketched on the board. Some filled entire pages with words they had learnt. Others simply drew pictures.

While collecting those sheets, I realised I should probably ask myself the same question. What do **I** actually remember from these twenty-eight days?

Surprisingly, it wasn't the worksheets we completed or the lesson plans we followed. It was the conversations during lunch breaks. The random inside jokes. Watching someone finally understand a concept they had struggled with for days. The girl asking whether she could take the colouring book home. The students asking whether they could "keep" Sanvi and Laher in the village. Those are the memories that stayed with me.

I signed up thinking this internship would teach me about rural India. It did, but probably not in the way I expected. Instead, it taught me about learning, curiosity, empathy, and the incredible complexity of teaching a young child. Somewhere over those twenty-eight days, I stopped thinking of teaching as simply delivering information. I started seeing it as understanding another human being well enough to help them take one step further than they could yesterday.

## Why I Wrote Every Day

One thing I'm especially glad I did was write something after almost every single day of the internship.

Initially, it was just supposed to be documentation so I wouldn't forget what happened. But after a while, the logs slowly became something else. I started writing less about **what** happened and more about **why** I thought it happened.

Why did one worksheet work while another completely failed? Why did one kid suddenly improve after changing one tiny thing? Why were Grade 6 students sometimes outperforming Grade 7? Why did repeating the same lesson twice make me a noticeably better teacher?

Looking back now, I think almost half of what I learnt happened while writing those reflections rather than while in the classroom. Writing forced me to slow down, revisit the morning, and search for patterns that I probably would've forgotten by the next day.

This blog would not have been possible without those daily logs. They preserved all the little moments that would otherwise have disappeared with time and gave me a chance to revisit them months later.

> I also like to sometimes think of writeups as photographs of your mind.

This was my best attempt to communicate what those twenty-eight days felt like. Consider this my blank sheet that was given to the kids on the last day.

If nothing else, all I can say with certainty is that I will look at the world a little differently now.
